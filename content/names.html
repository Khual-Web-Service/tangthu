Zomi, Zo, Chin, Kuki, Mizo and Lai: Names and Identity

What to call ourselves is a debated question in Zo communities [[go2008]]. This page does not settle it. It explains where each name comes from and why people hold to it.

{{short}}
  • Zomi and Mizo both mean “Zo people.” [[maninindia]] [[thangsuanhau]]
  • Chin and Kuki were first used by outsiders, but many people are proud of them today. [[carey1896]] [[lalhmingliana2018]]
  • In 1896 the British recorded three self-names: Yo in the north, Lai around Hakha, Shu in the south. [[carey1896]]
  • No name is “wrong.” Call people what they call themselves.
{{/short}}
A note on respect. Every name below is someone’s chosen identity. Use the name a community uses for itself. When writing about the whole family of peoples, this site uses “Zo” as a broad umbrella term, not as a ruling on which name is right.

Zo

Widely understood as the name of a common ancestor, from whom the Chin, Kuki, Mizo and Zomi tribes trace descent. [[maninindia]] The 1988 Champhai declaration described the Zo as “descendants of one ancestor,” living across the Chin Hills, the Arakan, the Chittagong Hill Tracts, Mizoram and Manipur. [[virthli]]

Zomi

“Zo people.” According to Thangsuanhau and Jamkhanpau, Sukte T. Haugou proposed the name at an American Baptist Missionary Fellowship meeting in Rangoon in 1950 and adopted for the Zomi Baptist Convention at Saikah in 1953. [[thangsuanhau]] Tedim communities were early adopters. [[wiki-tedim]] These are organisational milestones; they mark when the name was formally adopted, not when Zo identity began. [[brief2026b]] A formal proclamation of the name “Zomi” is dated 6 December 1988. [[go2008]] In Manipur, “Zomi” is used as a collective identity by groups including Paite, Simte, Vaiphei, Zou and others. [[thangtungnung]] Myanmar’s government never adopted the term, so “Chin” is often added alongside it. [[wiki-tedim]]

Mizo

“People of Zo.” Commentators note that Zomi and Mizo carry identical meaning, but the preferred form became politically contentious. [[wiki-zoro]] Mizo is the established name in Mizoram, where it became the state’s name.

Laimi

“Lai people.” Already in 1896 British officers recorded that the Tashon, Haka and more southern tribes called themselves Lai. [[carey1896]] One Zomi writer argues that “Lai” began as a local term around Hakha rather than a national name. [[suanzadong2023]] Many Lai speakers hold it as their own name. Both views are held in good faith.

Chin

The name used for the state in Myanmar, for Chin National Day and for many political and church bodies. Its origin is debated. Khup Za Go reports that many people called Chin do not regard it as their own name and some consider it degrading [[wiki-tedim]]; at the same time, Chin National Day, celebrated since 1948, shows how many people embrace “Chin” as a national name. [[bni-cnd]]

Kuki

A name long used for related peoples in Manipur, Assam and beyond, and in colonial records. In the Anglo-Kuki War of 1917–19 it named a common struggle against British rule, and it remains a strongly held identity. [[guite2019]] Since 2023 the compound “Kuki-Zo” has been widely used in Manipur, though some legislators have objected to the label. [[aljazeera2023b]] [[pti-dh2024]]

Zou

A distinct tribe of southern Manipur and the adjoining Chin border. Its war against the British is remembered as the Zou Gal. [[kipgen2020]] Some writers describe the Zou as part of the wider Kuki family [[sabrang]]; many Zou also identify under the Zomi umbrella. [[thangtungnung]] “Zou” is a tribe name and should not be conflated with the umbrella “Zo.”

For clan practice more widely, see Culture.

Beh and phung: clan and family lines {{oral}}

A beh is a patrilineal family line, traced through sons from a founding ancestor; the eldest son normally inherits and heads the line. [[nang2007]] A 2002 community list by Thang Lian Pau records about 200 beh names (for example Guite, Khupmu, Lunmu, Thangman, Hatlang) and twelve phung: Dim, Gangte, Khuano, Paite, Saizang, Sihzang, Simte, Teizang, Thahdo, Vaiphei, Vangteh and Zote. [[thangliampau2002]]

Source note: the list is one person’s compilation with no sources or definitions. Its phung mix names used elsewhere for tribes (Paite, Thahdo, Vaiphei) with area or village groups (Dim, Saizang, Sihzang), so it does not settle how the terms should be used. Families’ own genealogies are welcome.

Where do the names come from? {{sch}}

Historians separate the people’s own name, the endonym, from names given by neighbours and colonial officials, the exonyms. [[brief2026]] Two explanations are proposed for the name Zo, and neither is settled: an ancestor’s name, or a word for the uplands. [[vumson1986]] [[lehman1963]] In 1896 Carey and Tuck wrote that the Northern Chins called themselves Yo, Lai or Shu. [[carey1896]]

Historians separate the people’s own name (the endonym) from names given by neighbours and colonial officials (exonyms). [[brief2026]] In 1896 Carey and Tuck wrote that the tribes they designated “Chins” did not recognise that name: “The Northern Chins call themselves Yo, the Tashons, Haka, and more southern tribes Lai, while the Chins of Lower Burma give their name as Shu.” [[carey1896]] As early as 1874 a British officer’s Lushai grammar called the language “Dzo.” [[lewin1874]]

There are two main explanations of what Zo means:

  • An ancestor’s name. Indigenous historians such as Vumson, Sing Khaw Khai and Vum Kho Hau hold that Zo was a common forefather, as clan genealogies record. [[vumson1986]] [[singkhawkhai1995]] [[vumkhohau1963]]
  • A word for the uplands. The anthropologist F. K. Lehman connected zo to the cool highlands, in contrast with the warm lowlands. [[lehman1963]] Critics reply that the people used the name while they still lived in the valleys. [[brief2026]]

“Chin” came into English from Burmese, and its original meaning is disputed. [[zec-who]] “Kuki” was a term used by Bengalis, not by the people themselves [[lalhmingliana2018]]; it appears in English sources by the 1790s. [[rawlins]] A Zomi community page, following Vumson, says the British took the name “Chin” from the Burmese. It quotes Zomi scholar Hau Go calling the term “altogether foreign to us” and “derogatory.” [[zec-who]] One proposed origin links it to the Burmese word for “basket” [[zec-who]]; Carey and Tuck reported it was “said to be a Burmese corruption of the Chinese ‘Jin,’ or ‘Yen,’ meaning ‘man.’” [[carey1896]] The name “Lushai” appears in British Cachar reports around 1850, the Mizo Union was formed in 1946 [[lalengzuali2024]], and in 1954 Parliament renamed the Lushai Hills District the Mizo District. [[act1954]] After the 1871–72 Lushai Expedition, T. H. Lewin wrote that “the generic name of the whole nation is Dzo.” [[lalhmingliana2018]] The 1891 Burma census lumped the “Chins” together as a loose group of hill tribes. “Mizo” came into wide use in the 1940s, and some groups outside Mizoram who prefer Zomi, Laimi or clan names feel it leaves them out. [[lalhmingliana2018]] Neither “Chin” nor “Kuki” originated in Zo languages, yet both are now held with pride by many communities. Where a name came from does not decide whether it is legitimate today.

Why is the naming debate still open? {{sch}}

British records grouped these peoples as Kuki-Chin-Lushai, and much modern Zo writing tries to move beyond those categories. [[go2008]] Different umbrella names gained ground in different states for different reasons. Armed movements and the 1990s Kuki–Paite clashes are reported to have hardened boundaries in Manipur. [[satp-zra]] [[wiki-zra]] See The Colonial Era and the timeline for dates.
