Culture

Language, clan, festival, song and cloth carry Zo identity across generations and borders. This page is the one most in need of community voices.

Language {{doc}}

Zo languages belong to the Kuki-Chin branch of Tibeto-Burman. [[vanbik2009]] They are many, and often mutually intelligible only in part: in schools in Columbus, Ohio, for example, Lai Chin was reported as the common tongue among Chin students. [[unpo2012]] Tedim is classed as a Northeastern Kuki-Chin language. [[glottolog-tedim]]

Clans and chieftainship {{doc}}

Society was organised by clan and lineage. Political life varied: many northern and central communities had hereditary chiefs, while some southern Chin communities did not. Authority could also flow through land rights, marriage alliances, tribute and obligations between villages. [[lehman-inscr]] [[brief2026b]] The British retained the chiefs under indirect rule [[wiki-tlangpi]] until a mass meeting in Falam on 20 February 1948 voted to replace chieftain rule with democracy. [[bni-cnd]] Colonial gazetteers record histories of individual tribes such as the Sukte, Siyin, Lai and others. [[carey1896]]

Clan genealogies are best documented by families themselves. We welcome contributed genealogies (khangthu) with sources noted.

Festivals {{doc}}

Khuado (Khuado Pawi) is the Zomi harvest and New Year festival; the name begins with khua, “village” or “community.” [[wiki-khuado]] It is the biggest festival of the Zomi year. [[mitv2019]] It marks the end of the cultivation cycle and is usually celebrated in October. [[sharedcomm]] It is now held in the diaspora too, with communities in Australia gathering to pray for harvest and community health. [[dvb]]

Older Zomi festivals included a New Year rite before jhum cultivation, a first-fruits festival, and Mim Kut, the millet festival, which was more ritual than celebration. [[thangtungnung]]

Chin National Day (20 February) has become a major cultural as well as political celebration, at home and abroad. [[bni-cnd]]

Music {{doc}}

At one traditional Zomi festival, Haitungmut Kithoihna, the instruments played included the phit, tamngai, guate, taal and semgua, and the village priest blew a trumpet. [[thangtungnung]]

Dress and textiles

Cloth is woven on the backstrap loom. One end is tied to a fixed point and the weaver’s body holds the threads taut. Patterns are picked out with a porcupine quill, and a skilled weaver can take up to two months to make a traditional woman’s longyi. [[mcdougall2018]] Each area has its own palette, based partly on the plants available for dye:

  • Tedim (Zomi): stripes of black, green, white and red.
  • Falam: geometric designs in red, green, purply-blue and golden yellow.
  • Hakha (Lai): traditional designs, which one weaving business is now cataloguing.
  • Mizo: a white ground checked with black, red, yellow and green.

[[mcdougall2018]] Among the Paite, the puon dum (“black cloth”) is worn at condolences and formal occasions. [[ignca]] On the Chin textiles chosen for a Melbourne public artwork, the ngotekherh was once a men’s hunting cloth, and arrows on the bridal puanlaisen stand for courage and victory. [[maroondah]] One named motif, “a hen and her chicks,” is a large diamond surrounded by small ones. Today, cheaper machine-made cloth competes with handweaving, while weaving businesses in Chin State sell to the diaspora. [[mcdougall2018]]

Still needed: weavers’ own names for patterns and their meanings, especially from Zomi, Zou and Paite communities.
