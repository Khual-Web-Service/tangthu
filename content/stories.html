Zomi History Stories: Ciimnuai, Khai Kam and the Mautam Famine

History is carried by people and places: a city remembered in song, a chief who escaped across a river at midnight, a healer who dreamed an alphabet. [[wiki-khanthuam]] [[aeon2024]] Each story is labelled by what kind of evidence it rests on.

{{short}}
  • Ciimnuai: the village near Tedim remembered as the first home of the northern Zo. [[carey1896v2]]
  • Khai Kam: a Sizang leader who fought the British, gave himself up to save hostages, and was sent to prison on the Andaman Islands. [[wiki-khaikam]]
  • Pau Cin Hau: a healer who dreamed an alphabet in 1900. His script is now on phones and computers. [[aeon2024]] [[unicode7]]
  • Mautam: when the bamboo flowered in 1959, rats ate the crops and famine followed. [[newslaundry2015]]
{{/short}}

Ciimnuai, the remembered city {{oral}}

Ciimnuai is remembered as the first Zo settlement in the Chin Hills. The 1896 British gazetteer lists it as “Chinnwe,” six and a half miles south-west of Tedim, close to Saizang, and records it as “supposed to be the birth-place of the original Northern Chins.” [[carey1896v2]]

Guite tradition says the Guite house founded and ruled it. Their chronicle names Guimang I (around 1050) and Mangsuum I (around 1250), and says Geeltui, Kalzang and Tedim were founded from Ciimnuai. [[zam2018]] Thadou tradition gives a different account, crediting Song Thu with a migration around 1500. [[wiki-ciimnuai]] Some local historians think the name may once have belonged to earlier places outside the hills. [[wiki-ciimnuai]]

Dates from genealogy. These dates come from counting generations in a clan chronicle. They show how the Guite remember their past. They are not excavated or inscribed evidence, and other clans tell it differently. On how such records work, see How We Know.

Khampat and the great banyan {{oral}}

Many Mizo and Zo families remember Khampat, in the Kabaw Valley, as a home before the hills. A great banyan tree (bungpui) there is a symbol of that memory. [[vumson1986]] It still matters today. In 2016 India’s government asked Myanmar to help protect the tree, answering a long-standing Mizo request. A preservation committee and the Zo Reunification Organisation had campaigned for it. [[wire2016]]

{{photo:stories/khampat-bungpui}}

One scholar of Mizo memory notes that in folklore the ancestors planted the Khampat banyan before setting out on a migration that began at Zopui, west of the Tiau valley. The tree has become a unifying image of roots and shared land. [[chakraborty2010]]

Khan Thuam and the midnight ferry {{oral}}

Northern Chin tradition remembers Khan Thuam (died 1848), a Sukte chief (the “Kantum” of British records, who conquered much of the northern hills) raised by his widowed mother at Mualbem. [[wiki-khanthuam]] [[carey1896]] In one well-known episode, nine village headmen plot to kill him and he flees with his family. While he crosses a river by rope ferry at midnight, the rope is cut and he is carried downstream, but he survives. [[wiki-khanthuam]] [[khuppau2011]] His son Kam Hau later expanded the family’s power, eclipsing his brother. [[wiki-khanthuam]]

A Zolai account drawn from the tellings of Vung Za Kham and Pum Za Kham of Tonzang gives more detail. Khan Thuam was the son of Maangkim and Pi Lawmdim, orphaned young and bonded to the Limkhai chief Zelvum. In A.D. 1783 nine chiefs, from Saizang, Vangteh, Lamzang, Lophei, Kalzang, Thuklai, Khuasak, Buanman and Mualbem, plotted his death, and he fled with his family to Rallang under chief Khuangceu, where his son Kam Hau grew up. [[zomimsg-khanthuam]] Accounts differ on his sons: this one names five, while Thang Lian Pau lists seven (Luah Kai, Kam Hau, Pau Kam, Gawh Pau, Za Kai, Za Pau and Khan Zam). [[zomimsg-khanthuam]] [[thangliampau2010]]

Dates disagree: this site gives Khan Thuam’s death as 1848, Gin Khan Nang gives 1840, and Thang Lian Pau dates Kam Hau’s rule from 1820. [[wiki-khanthuam]] [[nang2007]] [[thangliampau2010]]

Kam Hau, Tedim and the Kamhau law {{oral}}

Kam Hau first settled Tedim around 1804, was driven back to Lamzang by raids, and resettled it in 1810 with Khoilam and Maanggin. The town grew to about 300 houses in five quarters (veng): Bonuai, Botung, Vaiphei, Sekzaang (Guite) and Khuangzaang (Thahdo). He came to rule 135 villages around Tedim and Tonzang. [[zomimsg-kamhau]]

From 1848 his council of eight (Kam Hau and Pauvum of the Sukte, Maanggin of the Hatlang, Khoilam and Telkhaat of the Hatzaw, Kimthuam of the Zillom, and Pau-am and Cinkim of the Samte) kept the Kamhau Upadi, a customary law. Expanded under Hau Cin Khup, it was printed in 1972 under Pum Za Mang with 25 chapters and 143 sections, in Zo and Burmese. [[zomimsg-kamhau]] Gin Khan Nang records an earlier printing by Pum Za Mang at Tonzang in 1925. [[nang2007]]

Thang Lian Pau gives the Kamhau line as Kam Hau (1820–1868), Khaw Cin (1868–1890), Hau Cin Khup (1891–1934) and Pum Za Mang (1934–1948), when chieftainship was abolished; separate lines ruled Mualbem and Saizang. [[thangliampau2010]]

How the Tedim-area groups got their names {{oral}}

Speculation, not established history: these are naming traditions retold in community magazines and collected in one undated essay. None has been checked against independent evidence, and some are folk etymologies. Read them as what people remember, not as proven fact. [[mangngaihcin]]

One retelling holds that the groups who left Ciimnuai took their names from places or chance events rather than from separate ancestors. [[mangngaihcin]]

  • Zo: in this telling, zo meant the high, cold uplands, as opposed to sim, the warm valleys. People who settled the coldest country near Balzang were called “the Zo side”, and the name stuck. The essay credits Rev. G. K. Dal for this account. [[mangngaihcin]]
  • Teizang: a tiger at Luika killed pigs, dogs and children. After it was killed the village was abandoned, and the families who resettled on a flat (zang) where a teising tree grew became the Teizang, near today’s Mualbem. The essay dates this to roughly 1500–1600, a guess. [[mangngaihcin]]
  • Saizang and Sihzang: Saizang is said to come from a riverside flat with many elephants (sai), and Sihzang from a salt spring (sihtui). The essay calls both village names, not tribe names. [[mangngaihcin]]
  • Vangteh: on 9 March 1889, the day British troops took Siallum, a villager measuring house posts (vang teh) was asked the village’s name and answered with what he was doing. The officer wrote it down. [[mangngaihcin]]
  • Diim: named after a large pool (dim) near today’s Dimpi, around 1500. A nearby spring was said to turn drinkers into tigers, until the British filled it in. [[mangngaihcin]]
  • Khuano: Hau Cin Khup gave villages to his relative Do Thawng. His steward always answered that he was going “to the Khuano circle”, and the thirteen villages, including Dimpi, Dimlo and Suangpi, took that name. [[mangngaihcin]]

The same essay says Tedim speech began as a mixed language when many groups crowded into Tedim town around 1857–60, and that J. H. Cope later based his 1919 Zolai primer on it. That is the essay’s claim; this site has not confirmed the dates. [[mangngaihcin]]

The essay’s wider origin story {{contested}}

Not enough evidence: the claims below are given as the essay states them. No historical, linguistic or archaeological evidence has been found that supports the China-era parts, and historians do not link the Zhou or Qin dynasties to the Zo. They are kept here because they are part of how some people tell the story. [[mangngaihcin]]
  • A Zo dynasty in China: the essay says the ancestor Zo descended from Japheth, son of Noah, and that the Zhou dynasty (1123–256 BC) was a “Zo” dynasty, under which Confucius taught the family and clan customs Zomi still keep. Not enough evidence; Zhou and Zo are not shown to be connected. [[mangngaihcin]]
  • Flight from the Qin: it says forced labour on the Great Wall under the Qin emperor drove many people south into Burma, the Karen first, then the Burmans, Zomi and Kachin. Not enough evidence. [[mangngaihcin]]
  • Why the name “Chin”: it says Burmans feared the newcomers were Qin (“Chin”) soldiers pursuing runaways, and called them Chin. It also lists four other explanations: Luce’s Burmese tu-nge-chin (“friend”), Cope’s ancestor Cin Hil (younger son of Zo, brother of Kip Mang), Lehman’s “basket”, and Carey & Tuck’s Chinese Jin/Yen (“man”). It concludes that “Chin” is not a Zo name. The Lehman and Carey & Tuck readings are documented on the Names page; the others are unverified. [[mangngaihcin]]
  • Central Burma: it says Zomi lived beside the Mon, Pyu, Kanyan and Thet, and that the Chin people grew from the Thet; that Pagan comes from Zo Pugam, Mount Popa from Pupa, and the Burmese coin dingga from Zo dangka; and that Pagan-era inscriptions (1044–1287) record them as Chin. It cites Ngulh Khaw Suan for old house sites and relics near Sibani, between Sagaing and Ava. Not enough evidence for the word origins or the relics. [[mangngaihcin]]
  • Kale-Kabaw valley: it says Zomi crossed the Chindwin into the Kalay and Kabaw valleys around AD 800, citing Dr Khen Za Sian, and that the Burmese named the Chindwin after them. A traditional date, not archaeology. [[mangngaihcin]]
  • Ciimnuai and Lailun: it says one group went to Lailun near Falam and became Lai-mi (Falam, Hakha, Matupi, Thantlang, south to Paletwa), while others reached Ciimnuai and kept the name Zomi; later movers became Paite, Thahdo and Mizo. It traces Zo, Sihzang and Thahdo to Za Hung’s sons Sek Tak and Suan Tak, citing Kham Do Nang. Oral tradition and genealogy. [[mangngaihcin]]

Why did Khai Kam resist, and what happened to him? {{doc}}

Khai Kam led Sizang resistance when British road-builders entered the hills. He surrendered on 16 May 1894 to save hostages, was exiled to the Andaman Islands, and was released on 14 May 1910 and came home. [[wiki-khaikam]] [[reid1893]] British records name Kaikam, the Siyin, among the leaders of the 1892 rising. [[carey1896v2]]

Khai Kam (1864–1919) of Khuasak led Sizang resistance when British road-builders entered the hills. British records name “Kaikam, the Siyin” among the leaders of the 1892 rising. [[carey1896v2]] On 7 December 1888 Sizang fighters attacked a road party. A combined force of Sizang, Kamhau and Sukte men kept fighting into 1889, and the British built Fort White on captured land. [[wiki-khaikam]] [[reid1893]] For the wider annexation, see The Colonial Era.

After a second rising in 1892, every Sizang chief but Khai Kam surrendered. On 16 May 1894, after the British took male relatives hostage, he gave himself up with his father and brother. He was sentenced to life and sent to the Andaman Islands. He was released on 14 May 1910 and came home. [[wiki-khaikam]] A university in Kalay was once named after him, and a sports centre in Hakha and a park near Kalaymyo carry his name. [[wiki-khaikam]]

Who was Pau Cin Hau? {{doc}}

From 1900, Pau Cin Hau reported dreams of the creator, Pathian, in which he was given a book of symbols. [[aeon2024]] By 1931 his movement is reported to have counted about 37,500 followers. [[aeon2024]] The full account, including the script and the missionary responses, is on Faith & Literacy.

What happened in the 1959 mautam famine? {{doc}}

Bamboo flowers every few decades, and the rats that multiply on the seed eat the crops. In 1959 the mautam brought famine to the Lushai Hills, and the district council’s request for help to Assam was refused. [[newslaundry2015]] Anger over the famine fed the Mizo National Front, which declared independence on 1 March 1966. [[newslaundry2015]]

Every few decades the region’s bamboo flowers all at once. Rats multiply on the seed, then eat the crops. In 1959 this mautam brought famine to the Lushai Hills. The district council asked Assam for help and was refused. [[newslaundry2015]] Anger over the famine fed the Mizo National Front. It declared independence on 1 March 1966, and on 5 March Indian aircraft struck Aizawl. [[newslaundry2015]] Peace came with the Mizo Accord of 1986. [[newslaundry2015]] Dates across the period are on the timeline.
